One building, four departments

The building is a quadrangle around a large central courtyard. It was planned for four ministries at once: the Foreign Office and India Office on the St James's Park side, and the Colonial and Home Offices facing Whitehall.

Scott designed the outside of all four. Matthew Digby Wyatt, the India Office's own surveyor, designed the India Office interiors, which is why that section looks so different inside.

The two park-side offices were decorated to impress foreign visitors. Scott called the Foreign Office "a kind of national palace or drawing room for the nation". The Whitehall offices were treated as working buildings and kept deliberately plain.

Arriving on King Charles Street

1

The King Charles Street gateway

South side · triple-arched entrance

The entrance is a triple-arched gateway in the centre of the King Charles Street front. The old Charles Street, a street of houses and shops, was demolished to make way for the new offices. In the 1770s Ignatius Sancho, born on a slave ship and later a celebrated letter-writer, ran a grocer's shop on Charles Street, roughly where the building now stands.

At the Whitehall end, an arch carries the building across King Charles Street to the Treasury block. At the park end, the steps down to Horse Guards Road are guarded by John Tweed's statue of Robert Clive. It was unveiled on Whitehall in 1912, moved here in 1916, and has been the subject of repeated calls for removal since 2020.

Point outThe view west along King Charles Street to Clive and the park, before you go in.

2

The Main Quadrangle

Centre of the building · no photography

Stand in the courtyard and you can see all four departments around you. This is the place to tell the story of the design.

The Foreign Office had been housed in a run of old houses in and around Downing Street. A design competition in 1856 was meant to replace them. Scott did not win it, but the result was not binding and he ended up with the job.

Scott was a Gothic Revival architect and drew a Gothic Foreign Office. The Prime Minister, Lord Palmerston, refused it and insisted on a classical design. Scott redrew the building in an Italianate style and later reused much of his Gothic scheme for the Midland Grand Hotel at St Pancras.

Point outTurn to face the south-west corner. That is the India Office, and the tour goes there first.

Section 1 · south-west

The India Office

From 1858 the British government ruled India directly through the Secretary of State for India and his council, who worked here from 1868. Wyatt designed the interiors to match the status of the job. When India became independent in 1947 the India Office ceased to exist, and the Foreign Office took over its rooms.

3

Durbar Court

Heart of the India Office · ground floor

Wyatt's masterpiece. It was built as an open courtyard, with three storeys of columns and arches on all four sides. The ground-floor Doric and first-floor Ionic columns are polished red Peterhead granite. The Corinthian columns at the top are grey Aberdeen granite. The floor is Greek, Sicilian and Belgian marble. The glass roof was added in 1868.

It was first used in 1867, before the building was finished, for a reception for the Sultan of Turkey during his state visit. It only became "Durbar Court" in 1902, when part of Edward VII's coronation celebrations was held here. A durbar is a royal court or ceremonial gathering.

Point outThe two colours of granite, and the busts of figures from British India set around the walls.

4

Up the Gurkha Stair

India Office · to the first floor

The tour climbs the Gurkha Stair to reach the Council Chamber. Look up as you climb: sculpted figures stand above the stairs.

5

The India Office Council Chamber

India Office · first floor

The Secretary of State for India and his council met here between 1868 and 1947. Wyatt brought the great doors, doorcases, furniture and marble chimneypiece from the Directors' Court Room of the East India Company's old headquarters at East India House on Leadenhall Street.

The chimneypiece is by the Flemish sculptor Michael Rysbrack and dates from 1730. The central panel shows Britannia by the sea receiving the riches of the East Indies. Asia leads a camel and Africa leads a lion, with the Thames as a river god and ships setting out behind.

For the Sultan's 1867 visit the room became a dining room for the most important guests. Reports said every item on the tables was gold. After 1947 it housed the Foreign Office's German Department and hosted the 1948 Three-Power Conference on Germany. Some early NATO discussions were held here in 1950.

Point outThe chairman's seat with the East India Company's crest, a lion rampant in a medallion.

6

Down the Muses Stair

India Office · towards the Foreign Office

This staircase once led up to the Secretary of State for India's own office. It is lit by an octagonal glass lantern, held up by figures of goddesses of plenty and cherubs representing Roman virtues.

Below hang portraits of Emperor Napoleon III and Empress Eugénie. They were given to the East India Company as thanks for its part in the 1855 Paris Exhibition.

Point outThe lantern from the bottom of the stair, then head north into the Foreign Office.

Section 2 · north-west

The Foreign Office

The first Foreign Secretary, Charles James Fox, was appointed in 1782, but the department had no purpose-built home until this one was finished in 1868. Scott designed it as a showpiece for receiving ambassadors, with state rooms for dinners, conferences and receptions.

7

Locarno Dining Room

Locarno Suite · next to the India Office

The middle room of the three, also used for meetings. It is best remembered as the room Lord Salisbury preferred to work in instead of the Secretary of State's own room. Two doors, made to match Scott's originals, now lead straight through into the former India Office.

When restorers took down the plasterboard and dirty red silk in the late 1980s, they found the original olive and gold stencilling underneath. It was damaged but complete enough to copy exactly.

8

Locarno Conference Room

Locarno Suite

A gilded ceiling on iron beams, finished in majolica. Round plaques carry the arms or emblems of 20 countries. This was the first room of the suite to be uncovered during restoration, and it went back to use as a conference room in summer 1990.

Point outAsk the group to find a country they know among the plaques.

9

Locarno Reception Room

Locarno Suite · overlooks the Main Quadrangle

The largest room was designed as a Cabinet Room but seems never to have been used as one. In the First World War the Contraband Department moved in, and by 1925 the rooms were too shabby, dark and draughty for daily work.

Then the Locarno Treaties, meant to settle Europe after the war, were initialled in Switzerland in October 1925. The delegates came to London to sign them on 1 December 1925, and this suite was the only suitable venue. Royal portraits were hung to hide the worn decoration. Press and newsreel cameramen were packed in tiers behind a barrier and perched above the windows.

The suite was redecorated and renamed after the treaties. From 1939 the chandeliers were covered and it became the Communications Department's cypher branch. After 1945 it was split into plasterboard cubicles for the legal advisers. The barrel-vaulted ceiling, with classical figures and the signs of the zodiac, had been scrubbed off in the 1920s. Restorers found one section that had only been painted over and rebuilt the design from it.

Point outThe zodiac ceiling. Almost all of it is a reconstruction based on one surviving patch.

10

The Grand Staircase

Foreign Office · the ambassadors' route

The main greeting point for visiting dignitaries. A bust of Charles James Fox, the first Foreign Secretary, looks over the stairs. George III detested Fox and was dismayed by his appointment. Above is a dome with the signs of the zodiac and female figures representing the nations Britain dealt with.

Around the walls are five murals by Sigismund Goetze, painted between 1912 and 1921 and given by the artist. They tell the story of the British Empire up to the League of Nations. Lord Curzon, then Foreign Secretary, objected to the nude figures and refused them. It took a Cabinet meeting under Lloyd George to approve them. Goetze's model for Britannia was Gertrude Brown, and her small daughter posed for Belgium carrying a broken sword.

The murals are now contested for their imperial imagery, especially Britannia Pacificatrix, which shows Africa as a small boy. In 2020 the shadow Foreign Secretary raised them with the government, and the department reviewed its artworks and added information panels.

Point outFox's bust, then the murals. This is a good place to ask the group what the building should do with art like this.

11

The Foreign Secretary's view

Park front · not usually on the route

On the evening of 3 August 1914, the day before Britain entered the First World War, Sir Edward Grey stood at the window of his room here looking over St James's Park. The gas lamps along the Mall were being lit. He told his friend J. A. Spender, editor of the Westminster Gazette, that the lamps were going out all over Europe and would not be lit again in their lifetime.

Point outThe park-side windows. Tell the story here, then walk east along the north side of the quadrangle.

Section 3 · north-east

The Colonial Office

The Whitehall half of the building was the second phase. Scott was appointed in 1868, the contract was signed in 1870 at £242,323, and staff moved in from August 1875. The Colonial Office took the northern half, towards Downing Street. Inside, both Whitehall offices were kept as plain as a major department of state allowed.

12

The Downing Street corner

North-east · seen from Downing Street and Whitehall

The corner pavilion facing Downing Street carries statues by Henry Hugh Armstead of four Colonial Secretaries: Sir William Molesworth, Lord Glenelg, Lord Ripon and Lord Bathurst. Along the Whitehall front, Armstead carved the five continents in the spandrels on the Colonial Office side.

Scott was never happy with this half. Costs were cut repeatedly, and Acton Ayrton, the new First Commissioner of Works, publicly attacked spending on painters, sculptors and architects. Scott said in 1872 that his design had been "greatly impoverished for economy's sake".

Point outThe four statesmen on the corner, if you leave by Downing Street or walk round afterwards.

Section 4 · south-east

The Home Office

The Home Office took the southern half of the Whitehall range, with part of it given to the Local Government Board from 1871. The Home Office stayed until 1978, when it moved to a new building. Its departure helped ease the overcrowding that had nearly doomed the whole building.

13

The Whitehall front

East side · fifteen bays facing Whitehall

Scott's Whitehall front was meant to have a central porch and end pavilions topped with 90-foot cupolas. In 1870 the Office of Works ordered them removed, and two corner towers were later cut too. What survives is the seated Queen Victoria with a lion and unicorn over the centre, and John Birnie Philip's carvings of the continents on the Home Office side.

Point outLook for the carved elephant on the Whitehall facade, which most people walk past.

Back at the gate

How the building was nearly lost

Two world wars and a growing workload packed the building with staff. After 1945 there was no money and little taste for Victorian decoration, so the grand rooms disappeared behind false ceilings and plasterboard partitions.

In the 1960s the government planned to demolish Scott's building as part of a rebuilt Whitehall. In 1963 the Foreign Secretary, Rab Butler, said the country could not afford "to stable a white elephant in Whitehall". A public outcry and a shortage of money stopped the plan.
